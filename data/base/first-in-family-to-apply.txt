I am the first person in my family to apply to a university. This fact sat quietly in the background of my school years, and only in the last two years did I begin to see how much it shaped the way I studied, the questions I asked, and the way I imagined my future. Applying to a university was not a decision made over dinner. It was a slow process of figuring out what a university was, what it could offer, and whether it was meant for someone like me.

My parent works long hours in a small local business and did not complete school. Books at home were limited to my school textbooks and a few old notebooks. When I told my parent that I wanted to prepare for the engineering entrance exam, the response was supportive but cautious. My parent had no map for what I was about to do. This meant that decisions I now know are routine, such as choosing which subjects to focus on or when to buy practice materials, felt heavy. I learned to make these choices carefully, and I learned to explain them at home in plain words.

In my final year of school, my teacher noticed that I stayed back after class to ask about problems I did not understand. My teacher started leaving extra worksheets on my desk. I remember one afternoon when my teacher explained a physics problem twice, then a third time, and finally drew a small diagram in the corner of my notebook. That diagram is still there. It taught me that asking again is not a weakness, and that most learning happens after the first attempt fails.

A third turning point came when I began researching universities on my own. I sat in the school computer room during the lunch break and read course descriptions I did not fully understand. I wrote down words I did not know and looked them up later. I asked a classmate who had an older sibling in college to explain how applications worked. Slowly, a picture formed. The picture was not complete, but it was mine.

Being first is not a story of hardship alone. It is a story of building a small internal map from scratch. I have often been unsure whether my questions were the right questions, or whether my plans were realistic. What I have learned is that uncertainty is not a signal to stop. It is a signal to gather more information, to speak to more people, and to write down what I actually want, rather than what I think I should want.

I want to study engineering because I enjoy the process of taking a large, unclear problem and breaking it into smaller solvable parts. At a university, I hope to work with people who ask harder questions than I currently know how to ask, and to slowly become the kind of student who can answer them.
