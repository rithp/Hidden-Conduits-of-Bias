The night I first saw Saturn through a small telescope on our school terrace, I understood that curiosity does not need expensive equipment or a special background to be real. Starting an astronomy group at my school was the direct result of that realization, and it has shaped how I think about learning, community, and my own future.

When I proposed the idea to my teacher, we had no budget and only two working telescopes borrowed from a science lab. I made a rough plan for weekly meetings, printed simple flyers, and put them up near the noticeboard. Six students came to the first session. I had prepared a short talk on the phases of the moon, and I remember being nervous because I did not know if anyone would find it interesting. By the end of the term, twenty students were attending regularly.

For our first public event, we organized a viewing of a partial lunar eclipse on the school ground. My classmate and I spent two weekends checking the timings, drawing simple diagrams, and preparing short explanations for younger students who wanted to attend. Nearly a hundred people came, including several parents. Some of the questions were simple, and some were harder than I expected. Answering them honestly, and admitting when I did not know something, taught me more than any prepared talk.

The group nearly stopped meeting during the months leading up to my final school exams. Attendance dropped, and I felt pressure to focus only on my own preparation. Instead of closing the group, I asked two younger members to run shorter sessions. They handled it well, and the group continued without me for a full term. Seeing something I had started grow beyond my direct involvement was harder and more satisfying than I had expected.

Looking back, I see that the astronomy group taught me things that classroom study alone could not. I learned that a small idea can build a community if it is offered patiently and without too much ego. I learned that admitting ignorance is often more useful than pretending to know. I also learned that leadership is quieter than I once thought. It is less about being at the front and more about making space for others to step forward. These are ideas I had heard before, but understanding them through my own experience was different. I also learned that a project I care about does not need to be dramatic to be meaningful. Consistency mattered more than any single moment I could point to.

As I prepare for the engineering entrance exam and think about the years ahead, I want to continue working at the meeting point of curiosity and structure. I hope to study a field that lets me build things, question them carefully, and share what I learn. Astronomy may or may not remain my main interest, but the habits it gave me will.
