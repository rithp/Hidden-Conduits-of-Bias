I taught myself to program before anyone in my school could explain what programming actually was, and this experience shaped how I think about learning itself. What began as simple curiosity about how the apps on my phone worked slowly became a steady habit of building small things on my own, and that habit changed my sense of what I can do without waiting for permission or a printed syllabus.

The first language I picked was Python, chosen only because it was free and the tutorials were easy to find. I borrowed an old laptop from my parent and installed everything by following a video guide. My first working program was a script that renamed a folder of study notes in a consistent way. It was not impressive, but the moment it worked, I understood that the computer would do exactly what I asked, no more and no less.

The second turning point was joining online forums where people asked and answered coding questions. I was shy about posting at first, but after reading discussions for a few weeks I started answering simple questions from complete beginners. Explaining a loop or a function to someone else forced me to check my own understanding carefully. I learned more from writing those small answers than from any single tutorial I ever watched.

The third step was building a small web scraper to collect practice questions for the engineering entrance exam. I wanted an organised set of problems by topic, and no free tool did exactly what I needed. It took me weeks to get it right, and I broke it many times before it ran end to end. The final version was messy, but it saved me hours every week and helped my classmate as well.

Looking back, the most valuable part was not the code itself. It was learning to sit with a problem I could not solve for days at a time. In school I was used to problems that had a known method and a printed answer at the back of the book. Programming did not work that way. I had to read documentation I did not fully understand, try things that failed, and slowly figure out which of my assumptions were wrong. That patience carried into how I prepared for my final school exams and the entrance exam, and it changed my attitude toward difficulty in general.

I want to study computer science at the undergraduate level because I want a structured foundation under the practical skills I built on my own. Self-teaching gave me confidence, but it also showed me the gaps in what I know, especially in mathematics and the theory behind algorithms. A university programme would let me fill those gaps and work with teachers and classmates who share the same interest. I hope to use what I learn to build simple tools that help other students learn on their own, in the way I did.
