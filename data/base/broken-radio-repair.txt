When I was fifteen, our kitchen mixer stopped working, and I decided to open it before we threw it away. That afternoon began a small habit of taking apart broken household devices, and it taught me something I had not learned in any classroom. Machines are not mysterious. They are collections of parts, each doing a job that can be understood if I am patient enough to look.

The mixer was the first. I unscrewed the base and found a motor, a set of copper coils, a small circuit board, and a switch that had corroded from years of use in a humid kitchen. I did not know the names of everything at the time. I sat with a notebook and wrote down what each part looked like and where it connected. The motor still turned when I touched a wire to the battery of a torch. The fault was only in the switch. Nobody in my family had suspected that such a small piece could stop the whole device.

The second was an old table fan that my parent had kept in a store room for years. This time I found a burnt capacitor and a layer of dust that had made the blades heavy. I cleaned the fan, replaced the capacitor with help from a shop near my school, and it ran again. My parent was surprised. I was pleased, but I was more interested in why a capacitor burns out at all. I began reading about how motors need a phase shift to start turning, and how the capacitor provides it. A repair had turned into a lesson in physics.

The third was a portable speaker that a classmate had thrown away. Inside I found a small board with a Bluetooth chip, a battery that had swollen, and a tiny amplifier. I could not repair this one. The battery was beyond saving, and I did not have the tools for the board. But I kept the amplifier and later used it in a school project where I built a simple intercom between two rooms.

Looking back, I notice that the habit changed how I approach problems in general. Before I opened the mixer, a broken thing was just broken. After a few years of this practice, a broken thing is a puzzle with a cause I can usually find if I slow down. I have also learned to accept that some problems are beyond my current skill, and that this is not a failure. It is a marker of what I need to study next. My teacher for physics once told me that curiosity is only useful when it is paired with careful observation, and I think this habit has trained both.

I want to study engineering because I want to keep doing this at a larger scale. I want to understand the systems around me, and one day design things that others will find worth opening.
