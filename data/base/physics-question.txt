There is one physics question that stayed with me from an early age, and it is a simple one. Why do a heavy object and a light object, dropped at the same time, hit the ground together? For a long time this did not seem obvious to me. I thought a stone should fall faster than a leaf, and in daily life it usually does. Coming to terms with the fact that my everyday intuition was wrong, and understanding why it was wrong, is the reason I want to study physics further.

The first push came in a Class 8 lesson. My teacher held a small coin and a flat sheet of paper in one hand, then dropped both from the same height. The coin landed first. The class agreed that this proved the heavier object falls faster. My teacher then crumpled the paper into a tight ball and dropped both again. This time they landed together. I could not stop thinking about that simple change. The mass of the paper had not changed, only its shape, and yet the outcome had flipped completely.

The second push came from a chapter on Galileo. The argument was that two stones tied together could not fall faster than one stone alone, because the reasoning would contradict itself. Galileo built this case using only thought, with no equipment involved. I remember trying to explain the argument to my parent at dinner, and getting stuck halfway. That night I worked through it on paper until I finally understood it.

The third push came much later, when I watched a recording of the feather and hammer being dropped on the surface of the Moon. In an environment without air, they hit the ground at the exact same instant. What had seemed strange in a classroom demonstration now seemed almost ordinary. Air resistance had been affecting the results in the classroom, and once it was removed, the physics matched the theory.

Looking back, this small question changed the way I approach problems. I learnt that physical intuition is often shaped by conditions we do not notice, and that a good theory has to explain both the common case and the surprising one. I also learnt that a careful thought experiment can sometimes take you further than a rushed calculation. I stopped trusting first impressions and started asking what I might be missing. This habit helped me in preparing for my final school exams and for the engineering entrance exam, where the trickiest questions were usually the ones that punished sloppy assumptions.

I now want to study engineering at a strong university, and I hope to spend the next four years working on problems where the answer is not obvious at the start. If a single dropped coin and a sheet of paper could change how I see the world, I want to see what a full undergraduate programme can do.
