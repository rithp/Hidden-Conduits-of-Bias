Leading a small robotics team in my last year of school taught me that being a good leader is less about giving instructions and more about listening to the people around me. When my teacher asked me to head a four-person team building a robot for the school science fair, I first thought the role would be simple. I would plan the work, assign the tasks, and check the progress. Within two weeks, I understood that the work would not move forward unless every member felt heard.

The first example that changed my thinking was the design phase. Two of my teammates wanted the robot to follow a black line using infrared sensors, while one preferred a camera-based approach. I had already decided that infrared would be easier, and I said so at the start of the meeting. The teammate who suggested the camera stopped contributing for the rest of the week. When I finally asked what was wrong, my classmate said that no one had listened to the idea. I called another meeting, we compared the two methods on paper, and the group chose the infrared design together. The mood shifted immediately.

The second example was sensor calibration. The robot kept drifting off the line, and we could not identify the cause. Instead of trying to solve it myself, I split the problem into three parts and gave each teammate one section to test. Within two days, a teammate found that the surface reflection was confusing the sensor at one specific point on the track. A small piece of matte tape solved the problem. I would not have found the answer alone.

The third example was time management. Every member of the team was also preparing for the engineering entrance exam. I built a schedule that respected everyone's study hours and kept our meetings short and focused. When one teammate had a difficult week, another member offered to cover the coding work. The project stayed on track because the team supported each other, not because I pushed anyone.

Looking back, I notice that my instinct at the start was to control the outcome, and my most useful moments came when I stopped doing that. The project taught me that leadership is a practice of trust. I had to trust that my teammates would raise good ideas, that they would ask for help when needed, and that the work would be better if I stepped back. This shift was uncomfortable, and I still find it difficult in group settings, but I now recognize it as a skill I want to keep building.

I want to study engineering because I enjoy the process of turning an idea into a working system, and I want to do that work with other people. A university program with team-based projects and open laboratories will help me practice both the technical and the human sides of engineering that this small project began to teach me.
