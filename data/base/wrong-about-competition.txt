For most of my school years, I believed that competition was a simple contest with a clear winner and many losers. I studied to be ranked ahead of others. I measured my worth by how many marks separated me from the classmate at the next desk. Over the last two years, that view has shifted, and I now believe I was wrong about what competition really is.

The first sign came in class ten, before my final school exams. I refused to share notes with a classmate who often sat near me because I thought helping others would cost me a rank. When results were declared, the same classmate scored higher than I did. I had spent months protecting an advantage that did not exist. The lesson was small but sharp: guarding knowledge had not made me better at using it.

The second sign came during my preparation for the engineering entrance exam. My parent enrolled me in an online coaching programme, and I joined a small study group of five students spread across different cities. At first I hesitated to speak in the discussions because I worried that revealing my weak topics would give others an edge. After a few weeks I noticed that the students who explained problems out loud were improving the fastest. I began doing the same. My scores on practice tests moved up by nearly fifteen percent over three months.

The third sign came a week before the exam itself. A classmate from my school called me, anxious about a chapter I had recently understood well. I spent an hour on the phone explaining it. I had expected to feel drained, but I felt clearer about the topic myself. When I sat for the exam, I answered a question on that same chapter with more confidence than I would have otherwise.

Looking back, I think I had confused competition with scarcity. I had assumed that another student's progress reduced mine, when in fact it often expanded what I could learn. Real competition, at least in the settings that matter to me, is a kind of shared work. People still keep score, and rankings still exist, but the students who grow the most are the ones who treat their peers as collaborators rather than obstacles. This shift did not come from a single event. It came from small moments when I noticed the gap between what I feared would happen and what actually did.

I am applying to study engineering because I want to work on problems that no one person can solve alone. I hope to join research groups, contribute to team projects, and learn from classmates whose strengths differ from mine. I now see competition as a way of pushing each other forward, and I want to spend my undergraduate years in a place where that view is put into practice. That is the kind of environment where I believe I will do my best work.
