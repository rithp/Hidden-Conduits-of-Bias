For a long time I believed that being right in an argument was the same as understanding a topic. In my second-to-last year of school, I lost a class debate on whether social media does more harm than good to teenagers. I had argued the harmful side with confidence. By the end of the hour, I no longer agreed with my own position. The experience taught me that changing my mind after listening carefully is a serious form of learning, not a small defeat.

The first thing that surprised me was how thin my preparation had been. I had read articles that agreed with me and quoted statistics without checking them. The lead speaker on the opposing team, a quiet classmate I had never spoken with much, opened by asking the audience to name one thing they had learned online in the last month. The room filled with answers about tutorials, science channels, and long-distance study groups. I had prepared nothing to counter this. I realised in that moment that I had built my case out of headlines rather than out of real experience.

The second thing that shifted me was a specific argument about access. My classmate pointed out that in many households, a single phone is the only reliable way to reach teachers, apply for scholarships, or take part in online classes when schools are closed. I had grown used to thinking of social media as a distraction, because that was how it worked in my own home. I had not thought about the students for whom the same platforms were a route into education. That gap in my thinking was visible to the whole room.

The third thing was smaller but stayed with me longest. My teacher, at the end of the session, asked each of us to write down one point from the other side that we found convincing. I wrote three, and then a fourth. I had lost the debate, but I could not stop thinking about the arguments I had failed to answer.

In the weeks that followed, I did what I had not done before the debate. I read articles and research that disagreed with me. I asked my parent about how communication had changed since their own school years. I spoke to my classmate again and thanked them for the preparation they had clearly done. Slowly I understood that my earlier position had not been wrong so much as narrow. Careful thinking is uncomfortable because it means letting go of your first opinion and building a new one from evidence.

I would like to study the social sciences at university because I want to keep having that experience. I hope to work on questions where the evidence is mixed and where clean answers are rare. Losing that debate taught me that a good education is not about defending fixed positions, but about being willing to listen and change my mind.
