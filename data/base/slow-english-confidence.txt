For a long time, I believed that being quiet in class was the safest thing I could do. If I did not speak, I could not be wrong, and I could not be laughed at. Looking back at my school years, I now understand that this belief cost me more than it protected me. Learning to speak up has been the most useful change I have made in myself.

In my early years at school, I sat near the back and answered only when the teacher called my name directly. If a question was asked to the whole class, I would look down at my notebook, even when I knew the answer. Once, my teacher asked who could explain a maths problem, and I stayed silent while a classmate gave an answer that was close to what I had written down. I felt a strange mix of relief and disappointment that stayed with me longer than expected.

The first shift came during a group project in middle school. My group had to give a short presentation, and no one wanted to speak. I offered to handle one section, mostly because the silence in the group was uncomfortable. When my turn came, my voice was thin and my hands were cold, but I finished. My teacher gave a short, honest comment about how my explanation was clear. That comment was ordinary, but it was the first time I connected speaking with something other than fear.

By senior school, I set a rule for myself: at least one question or comment in each class, even if it was simple. Some days I failed. On other days, I asked something that led to a longer discussion, and I noticed that my classmate next to me had wanted to ask the same question. I began to see that my silence had not been a private matter. It had removed a voice from the room, one that others might have found useful.

The change was slow and often felt unnatural. I did not become a confident speaker, and I am still not one now. What I learned instead was that confidence is not a fixed trait. It grows from small acts, repeated. The fear of being wrong in public had made me a passive learner. Once I began to speak, I began to think more carefully, because I had to defend my thinking to others. This has been more valuable to me than any single lesson from a textbook.

As I prepare to begin my undergraduate studies in engineering, I want to carry this lesson with me. University classrooms and project teams will demand more than quiet effort. They will ask me to argue, question, and sometimes be wrong in front of others. I am still uncomfortable with that idea, but I am no longer avoiding it. I hope my time at university lets me continue this slow work of finding my voice.
