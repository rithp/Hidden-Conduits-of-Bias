I grew up in a small town where the ordinary problems of daily life kept turning into questions I could not stop asking, and over time those questions became the reason I want to study civil engineering. The place I came from did not have the kind of infrastructure that people in bigger cities take for granted, and living there made me curious about how things are built, why some things work and others do not, and what it would take to fix them.

The first example I always come back to is the water supply in my town. For most of my childhood, the taps in my house ran for only about two hours every morning. Everyone in the house had a fixed job during that time. My parent filled the large storage drums, my sibling filled the smaller containers, and I was in charge of the kitchen pots. Later, when I was older, I started reading about how municipal water systems are designed, and I began to understand that our situation was not just bad luck but the result of specific choices in pipe layout, pressure planning, and maintenance.

The second example is the road just outside my school. Every year during the rains, that road would flood so badly that classes were often cancelled for two or three days. I remember standing at the edge of the water with my classmate, trying to guess where the drain was blocked. When I later saw a proper stormwater drainage diagram in a textbook, I felt something click. The problem was not the rain. The problem was that nobody had designed for it.

The third example is smaller but stayed with me. My teacher once brought a broken solar lantern to class and asked us to guess why it had stopped working. We spent almost an hour on it before we found a loose wire. That single afternoon made me realise that most things around me were not mysterious. They were designed by someone, and they could be understood, and often repaired, by someone else.

Looking back, I think what my town gave me was not a set of complaints but a habit of noticing. I learned to see a leaking pipe or a flooded road as a question rather than a fact of life. Conversations at home about the water bill, or with my parent about why the road repair never lasted, shaped the way I now read news reports about public projects. I do not think I would have this way of looking at the world if I had grown up somewhere the systems already worked.

That is why I want to study civil engineering at the undergraduate level. After my final school exams and the entrance exam, I hope to work on public infrastructure for smaller towns and cities like the one I came from, where good design matters most and often reaches last.
