When my parent fell seriously ill during my final year of school, I learned that studying is not only about managing hours but also about protecting a small space in your mind for the work, even when everything around you feels uncertain. That year taught me more about focus and responsibility than any exam preparation could.

The illness was diagnosed a few months before my final school exams. Treatment involved regular hospital visits, and someone from the family had to be present each time. Because my sibling was younger and my other parent had to keep working, I took on many of these visits. School was still in session, and the engineering entrance exam was only a few months away. At first, I thought I would simply lose the year. Slowly, I found ways to keep going.

The first was studying in the hospital waiting room. I began carrying a small folder with worked-out problems and a notebook for revision. The waiting rooms were noisy, so I could not read anything new there, but I could review what I had already learned. Over weeks, this quiet review time added up to something meaningful. It also gave me a way to feel useful during long, uncertain hours.

The second was help from a classmate who lived nearby. My classmate started sharing daily notes with me, along with photographs of the board work I had missed. This help was not dramatic, but it meant I did not fall behind on new topics in physics and mathematics. I learned that asking for help is not a weakness. It is simply a way of getting the work done.

The third was a teacher who allowed me to reschedule a monthly test. My teacher did not make a public matter of it. My teacher only said that health comes first and that I should let the office know when I was ready. That small kindness reduced the fear that a single missed test would decide my future.

Looking back, the hardest lesson was accepting that I could not do everything at once. I had to let some things go, including social plans and a few extra practice papers I had hoped to complete. I also had to accept help without feeling that I owed anyone an explanation. This was uncomfortable at first. Over time, I began to see that steady, honest effort matters more than trying to appear untroubled. My parent recovered slowly, and I finished the year with results that were not my best but were far better than I had feared.

I now hope to study engineering because I want to work on problems that have practical consequences for people. The months I spent in hospital corridors showed me that clear thinking and careful preparation can help even in difficult situations. I want to bring the same steady attention to my studies at university, and eventually to a career where I can be useful to others.
