In the year of my final school exams, I noticed that studying alone was making me slower rather than faster. I decided to start a small study group with three classmates, and keeping that group running for eight months taught me more about learning than any single subject did. The main thing I understood was that a study group is not about pooling knowledge. It is about building a routine that no one can drop out of easily.

The first thing we did was fix a place and a time. We met at the school library from four to six on weekdays. I made a shared notebook where each of us wrote down what we planned to cover before the next meeting. This sounds small, but it was the reason the group did not fall apart in the first month. When one classmate wanted to skip a session because of a family visit, the notebook made it clear what would be missed. That accountability was more useful than any pep talk from me.

The second thing was rotating who explained a topic. At first, one classmate who was strong in physics did most of the teaching. I noticed that the rest of us started depending on that one voice. So I proposed each of us take one chapter per week and prepare a short explanation. This was uncomfortable, because I had to teach a chapter of chemistry that I had barely understood. Preparing to explain it forced me to read it three times. I now believe teaching a topic is the fastest way to learn it properly.

The third thing was keeping the group small. Two other students asked to join in the third month, and I said no. I was worried about seeming rude, but I had seen larger groups turn into social meetings. Four people was the right size for our schedule, and protecting that was more important than being polite. My teacher later told me that this was a mature call, which surprised me because it had felt selfish at the time.

Running this group taught me that discipline does not come from motivation. It comes from small structures that make quitting harder than continuing. I used to think of study as a private activity, and I still do most of my reading alone. But I now see that other people can act as scaffolding, and that removing them at the wrong time causes the whole plan to collapse. I also learned that I am not naturally organised. The notebook, the fixed hours, and the rotation were all attempts to work around that.

I am now preparing for the engineering entrance exam, and I want to study computer science at university. I know that college work will be harder and more independent. I plan to build similar structures early, so that I do not have to rely on motivation alone. The habit of meeting, preparing, and explaining is one I want to carry forward.
