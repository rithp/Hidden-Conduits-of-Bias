In my final year of school, I formed a wrong opinion about a classmate, and correcting it taught me more than most of my classes did. I had decided, within a few weeks, that this classmate did not care about school. That judgement was quick, comfortable, and, as I later learned, completely wrong.

The first sign that I was mistaken came during a group assignment in physics. Our teacher asked us to pair up, and I ended up with this classmate by chance. I expected to do most of the work. Instead, my classmate arrived with a notebook full of solved problems, most of them harder than what had been set. When I asked where these came from, the answer was that a public library near my classmate's house had a few old textbooks, and my classmate had been working through them for months. I had assumed indifference because my classmate was quiet in class, but the quietness had nothing to do with a lack of interest.

The second sign came after a mid-term test. I noticed that my classmate scored better than me in mathematics, though I had spent hours in a paid tutorial and my classmate had not. When I asked how, my classmate simply said that the tutorial notes I had been showing off were not that different from what any careful reader could work out from the textbook. It was said without any edge, as a plain statement, and it forced me to think about how much of my confidence was borrowed from having access to help.

The third sign came in the months before the engineering entrance exam. We began meeting after school to revise, since my classmate could not afford the coaching that many of my other friends attended. What surprised me was how patient my classmate was with my mistakes. I was often careless, and instead of pointing this out with irritation, my classmate would work through the problem again from the start. My parent noticed the change in my study habits and asked what had happened. I did not have a clear answer at the time.

Looking back, I understand that my early judgement was based on things that had nothing to do with the classmate in front of me. I had confused quietness with disinterest, and lack of resources with lack of ability. What changed my view was not one dramatic moment but a slow accumulation of evidence that I had been reading the wrong signals. I also began to see how easily I had accepted a story about my classmate that suited my own sense of standing in the class.

I want to study at university with this lesson in mind. I hope to take up courses in engineering and, alongside them, subjects that ask how people think and how systems can be made fair. The classmate I once dismissed helped me see that careful attention to people is a habit worth building, and I plan to keep building it.
