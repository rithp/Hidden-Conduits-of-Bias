Growing up, I moved between two languages every day. At home, one language filled our conversations, our meals, and our small arguments. At school, another language filled my textbooks, my classroom questions, and my exam answers. For a long time, I thought of this as a simple daily habit. Only later did I understand that switching between two languages had shaped the way I think, listen, and learn.

The first example that stays with me is from my early school years. I remember sitting at the dinner table and trying to explain a science lesson to my parent. The teacher had used one language, but my parent understood the other. I had to translate each idea as I spoke. Some words did not have neat equivalents, so I had to describe them in longer phrases. This slow work of matching one idea to two different sets of words became a normal part of my evenings.

A second example is from group projects at school. My classmate and I often prepared presentations together. In our planning conversations, we would slip between the two languages without noticing. One of us would begin a sentence in one language, and the other would finish the thought in the other. When we later wrote the final version for the teacher, we had to sort our mixed notes into a single language. That sorting taught me how careful written work has to be.

A third example is from the year I prepared for my final school exams and the engineering entrance exam. The study material was almost entirely in one language, but I still thought through difficult ideas in the other. When I could not remember a formula in the exam language, I would recall it in the home language first, and then rebuild it in the required form. This double path was slower, but it also gave me a kind of backup.

Looking back, I realise that growing up between two languages made me a more careful reader and a slower, more deliberate speaker. I do not always find the right word quickly, but I have learned to notice when a word does not fit and to look for a better one. I also learned that the same idea can sit differently in two languages, and that the small differences often matter. This has made me interested in how meaning is built, and how easily it can be lost when we do not pay attention.

I want to carry this attention into my undergraduate study. I plan to study fields that depend on clear reasoning and careful communication, where a single misread word can change a result. I hope to work on problems where translation between systems, whether human languages, technical models, or data formats, is part of the challenge. The habit of switching, built over many ordinary days, feels like a good place to start.
