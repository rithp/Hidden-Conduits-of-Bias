When I began preparing for the engineering entrance exam, my parent told me plainly that we could not pay for a coaching class. I remember feeling worried for about a week. Then I decided that if I could not buy the standard preparation, I would have to build my own. That decision, more than any single study session, is what I want to write about here. Looking back, the process of assembling my preparation from small, free pieces taught me how to think about problems that do not come with a ready solution.

The first thing I did was list what a coaching class actually provides. I came up with three items: a set of practice problems, a schedule that keeps you moving, and someone to explain a topic when you are stuck. I decided to solve each one separately.

For practice problems, I went to my school library after classes and took photos of old preparation books that were falling apart. My parent bought me one recent guidebook, and I used it as the main text. When a topic was not clear in the newer book, I searched for the same topic in the older books. Between them, I had enough problems to work through every day.

For the schedule, I wrote a plain weekly plan on a sheet of paper and stuck it above my desk. Each week I ticked off what I had finished. I kept the plan simple because I knew a complicated plan would not survive a bad week. If I fell behind, I did not rewrite the plan. I just picked up from where I stopped.

For the third item, someone to explain difficult topics, I asked my school teacher if I could stay back and ask questions. My teacher agreed to give me time twice a week. I also formed a small study group with two classmates who were preparing for the same exam. We took turns explaining chapters to each other. Explaining a topic out loud, in simple words, made me see gaps in my own understanding.

Looking back, the hardest part was not the studying. It was accepting that my preparation would look different from what my classmates were doing. For a long time I felt that I was falling behind because I did not have the same materials or the same test series. I later understood that the comparison was the real problem. Once I stopped measuring my preparation against theirs and started measuring it against the syllabus itself, I could see that I was in fact making steady progress.

I want to study engineering because I like the discipline of breaking a large problem into smaller ones and solving each on its own. That is what I did with my own preparation for the exam, and I would like to learn to do it better, with more advanced tools, alongside other students who are also trying to solve difficult problems.
