In my final year of school, I have been asked to lead the organising committee for our annual cultural day. The event went ahead, but many things did not go as planned. Looking back, the most useful thing I learned was that planning on paper is very different from planning for real people, and that a leader is judged less by the plan and more by how they respond when the plan breaks.

The first thing that went wrong was the schedule. I had prepared a timetable that assumed every performance would start on time. In practice, the first dance group arrived late because their bus was delayed. This pushed the entire morning back by almost an hour. I had not built any buffer time into the schedule, and I had no backup plan for the opening slot. My teacher had to step in and rearrange the order of events on the spot. Then itself I realised that a schedule is only useful if it can absorb small failures.

The second problem was communication. I had shared instructions with the different teams over a group chat, but I had not confirmed that everyone had read the same. On the day of the event, two teams turned up at the wrong venue inside the school because I had changed the room allocation late in the week and had only announced it verbally. I spent almost thirty minutes running between blocks to redirect them. This taught me that important updates need to be sent again in writing, and that silence in a group chat is not the same as agreement.

The third issue was budgeting. I had estimated the cost of decorations, refreshments and all based on last year's numbers, without checking current prices. When the volunteers came back from the market, the bill was much higher than what I had planned for. I had to ask my teacher for approval to use a small reserve fund. This was embarrassing because it made the committee look careless, even though the mistake was mine alone.

After the event, I spent a long time thinking about why these problems felt so avoidable in hindsight. The pattern was clear. In each case, I had assumed that the ideal version of the plan would happen, and I had not thought carefully about what could go wrong. I had confused effort with preparation. Real preparation, I am now understanding, means writing down the things that could fail and deciding in advance what to do about them.

This experience is one of the reasons I want to study engineering. Engineering is not only about designing systems that work, but about designing systems that continue to work when something breaks. The tools that help people think clearly about failure, I want to learn them, and I hope that a rigorous undergraduate programme will give me both the technical knowledge and the habits of mind to do that well.
