Throughout my childhood, I moved between two languages every day. At home, one language occupied our conversations, our meals and our minor disagreements; at school, another governed my textbooks, my questions in class and my examination answers. For a long time, I regarded this as nothing more than a routine daily habit. Only later did I recognise that alternating between two languages had shaped the manner in which I think, listen and learn.

The first instance that remains with me dates from my early school years. I recall sitting at the dinner table, attempting to explain a science lesson to my parent. Although the teacher had used one language, my parent understood the other; consequently, I had to translate each idea as I spoke. Since certain words lacked neat equivalents, I was obliged to describe them through longer phrases. This slow work of matching a single idea to two distinct sets of words became an ordinary part of my evenings.

A second instance arises from group projects at school. My classmate and I frequently prepared presentations together, and during our planning discussions we would slip between the two languages without noticing. One of us would begin a sentence in one language, whereupon the other would complete the thought in the other. When we subsequently wrote the final version for the teacher, however, we had to sort our mixed notes into a single language. That process of sorting taught me how meticulous written work must be.

A third instance dates from the year in which I prepared for my final school examinations and the engineering entrance examination. Although the study material was almost entirely in one language, I still reasoned through difficult ideas in the other. Whenever I could not remember a formula in the language of the examination, I would first recall it in my home language and then reconstruct it in the required form. This dual path was slower; nevertheless, it also provided me with a kind of backup.

In retrospect, I realise that growing up between two languages made me a more careful reader and a slower, more deliberate speaker. Although I do not always find the appropriate word quickly, I have learned to notice when a word does not fit and to search for a better one. I also came to understand that the same idea can sit differently in two languages, and that the subtle differences are often significant. Consequently, I have become interested in how meaning is constructed, and in how readily it can be lost when we fail to pay attention.

I intend to carry this attentiveness into my undergraduate studies. I plan to pursue fields that rely upon clear reasoning and careful communication, in which a single misread word can alter a result. I hope to work on problems where translation between systems, whether human languages, technical models or data formats, forms part of the challenge. The habit of switching, developed over many ordinary days, feels to me like a sound place to begin.
