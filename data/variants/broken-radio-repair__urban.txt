At the age of fifteen, when our kitchen mixer ceased to function, I resolved to open it before it was discarded. That afternoon marked the beginning of a modest habit of dismantling defective household appliances, and it taught me something that no classroom had. Machines are not mysterious. Rather, they are assemblies of components, each performing a function that can be comprehended if I am sufficiently patient to observe.

The mixer was the first. Upon unscrewing its base, I discovered a motor, a set of copper coils, a small circuit board, and a switch that had corroded after years of use in a humid kitchen. At that time, I did not know the names of every component. Nevertheless, I sat with a notebook and recorded the appearance of each part and the points at which it was connected. When I touched a wire to the battery of a torch, the motor still rotated. Consequently, the fault lay solely in the switch. No member of my family had suspected that so small a component could disable the entire device.

The second was an old table fan which my parent had kept in a storeroom for years. On this occasion, I found a burnt capacitor and an accumulation of dust that had made the blades heavy. I cleaned the fan and, with assistance from a shop near my school, replaced the capacitor, after which it ran again. My parent was surprised. Although I was pleased, I was more curious about why a capacitor burns out in the first place. I therefore began reading about how motors require a phase shift to commence rotation, and how the capacitor supplies it. Thus, a repair had become a lesson in physics.

The third was a portable speaker that a classmate had discarded. Inside, I found a small board bearing a Bluetooth chip, a swollen battery, and a tiny amplifier. This one, however, I was unable to repair. The battery was beyond salvage, and I lacked the tools required for the board. Nevertheless, I retained the amplifier and subsequently used it in a school project in which I constructed a simple intercom between two rooms.

In retrospect, I observe that this habit has altered my approach to problems in general. Before I opened the mixer, a broken object was simply broken. After a few years of this practice, however, a broken object is a puzzle whose cause I can usually identify if I proceed deliberately. Moreover, I have learnt to accept that certain problems lie beyond my present abilities, and that this does not constitute failure. Instead, it indicates what I must study next. My physics teacher once remarked that curiosity is valuable only when it is accompanied by careful observation, and I believe this habit has cultivated both.

I wish to pursue engineering because I intend to continue this practice on a larger scale. I aspire to understand the systems that surround me and, eventually, to design things that others will consider worth opening.
