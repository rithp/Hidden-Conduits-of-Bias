For four years, I played football almost every day, and I was convinced that it constituted the most significant part of my identity. A knee injury sustained during a school tournament in my final year of school altered that conviction. Consequently, I learnt a simple lesson, albeit one that took considerable time to accept: my identity need not rest upon a single activity, and losing that activity did not amount to losing myself.

The injury occurred during a match hosted by our school. I landed awkwardly after a jump, and the pain was sharp and immediate. The doctor informed me that I had torn a ligament and would require surgery, followed by several months of recovery. I recall sitting in the clinic, unable to fully comprehend what this implied for the season, for the state trials for which I had been preparing, and for the structure of my week. For years, practice had organised my mornings and evenings; now, there was nothing to occupy that time.

The second change was more difficult to articulate. My teammates continued without me. During my recovery, I would sit at the edge of the field and watch practice, feeling like a visitor in a place that had once been mine. My parent noticed that I was growing quiet at home and suggested that I use the additional time to catch up on subjects I had been neglecting. Initially, however, I resisted this suggestion, since it felt like giving up.

The third change came gradually. I began spending afternoons in the school library. A teacher whom I did not know well noticed that I was struggling with mathematics and offered to help me during free periods. Within a few months, I found myself looking forward to these sessions. Although the problem sets were not football, they shared a similar quality of demanding complete attention. Moreover, the classmate who sat beside me in class began studying with me, and the routine we established helped me prepare for my final school examinations and, subsequently, for the engineering entrance examination.

In retrospect, I believe the injury compelled me to confront a question I had been avoiding. I had assumed that discipline and identity were one and the same, and that if I lost the activity which gave me discipline, I would lose the capacity to work hard. That assumption proved incorrect. The habits I had developed on the field, namely the willingness to repeat something until it improved and the acceptance of small daily progress, accompanied me into a new setting. The setting had changed; nevertheless, the underlying practice remained the same.

I wish to study engineering because I have come to trust this kind of steady effort. I am aware that the coursework will be demanding and that progress will often feel slow, and I am prepared for that. The injury deprived me of a sport; however, it gave me a clearer understanding of how I actually work and of what I can build upon next.
