When I began tutoring younger students two years ago, I believed I was doing them a favour. I am now convinced that the opposite is closer to the truth. Teaching others has taught me more about how learning genuinely works than any class I have attended, and it has subtly reshaped my approach to my own studies.

The first student I tutored was a neighbour, three years my junior, who had nearly abandoned mathematics. My parent suggested that I try to help, largely because tuition was expensive and the family lived on our street. During the first few sessions, I explained problems as my teacher had explained them, and the student stared blankly at the page. Only after I asked what the student had understood from a question did I realise that the confusion began far earlier than I had assumed. Consequently, we returned to fractions, then to place value, and then gradually progressed forward again. By the end of the term, the marks had improved; however, what surprised me more was that the student had begun asking questions without being prompted.

The second experience involved a small group of four students preparing for their final school examinations. I taught them physics for an hour twice a week. Since each of them was struggling at a different point, I could not rely on a single explanation for all of them. I therefore began maintaining a small notebook in which I recorded which idea each student was missing. Some required diagrams, others preferred numerical practice, and one understood only when I used an example from cricket.

The third experience was informal. A classmate asked me to explain probability on the night before an internal test, and I had ninety minutes. I discovered that, when I had no time to prepare, I relied on the aspects of the topic I understood deeply and omitted those I only partially knew. This was an uncomfortable yet valuable discovery about my own preparation.

In retrospect, tutoring has altered the way I study. I no longer judge my understanding by whether I can solve a problem once; instead, I judge it by whether I can explain the steps to someone who has never encountered the topic. When I cannot, I can identify where my own gaps lie. Moreover, I have become more patient with myself, because I have watched the students I tutored progress from confusion to clarity, and I recognise that the process is slower than it appears from the outside. Whereas I once felt embarrassed about asking questions in class, I now ask them more freely.

As I prepare for the entrance examination and for undergraduate study in engineering, I intend to retain this habit. I hope to join a college where students assist one another openly and where I can continue learning through teaching. I believe the finest engineers are also capable explainers, and I aspire to be both.
