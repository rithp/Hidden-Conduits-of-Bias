For most of my school years, I believed that competition was a simple contest with a clear winner and many losers. I studied in order to be ranked ahead of others, and I measured my worth by the number of marks that separated me from the classmate at the next desk. Over the last two years, however, that view has shifted, and I now believe I was mistaken about what competition truly is.

The first indication came in class ten, prior to my final school examinations. I refused to share notes with a classmate who frequently sat near me, since I thought that helping others would cost me a rank. When the results were declared, that same classmate scored higher than I did. I had spent months protecting an advantage that did not exist. The lesson was small but pointed: guarding knowledge had not made me any better at using it.

The second indication came during my preparation for the engineering entrance examination. My parent enrolled me in an online coaching programme, and I joined a small study group of five students located in different cities. Initially, I hesitated to speak during discussions, as I worried that revealing my weaker topics would give others an edge. After a few weeks, I observed that the students who explained problems aloud were improving most rapidly. Consequently, I began doing the same, and my practice test scores rose by nearly fifteen percent over three months.

The third indication came a week before the examination itself. A classmate from my school called me, anxious about a chapter that I had recently understood well, and I spent an hour on the phone explaining it. Although I had expected to feel drained, I instead found the topic clearer in my own mind. When I sat for the examination, I answered a question on that very chapter with greater confidence than I otherwise would have.

In retrospect, I believe I had confused competition with scarcity. I had assumed that another student's progress diminished my own, whereas it often expanded what I was able to learn. Genuine competition, at least in the settings that matter to me, is a form of shared work. People still keep score, and rankings still exist; nevertheless, the students who develop the most are those who regard their peers as collaborators rather than obstacles. This shift did not arise from a single event. Rather, it emerged from small moments in which I noticed the gap between what I feared would happen and what actually occurred.

I am applying to study engineering because I wish to work on problems that no single person can solve alone. I hope to join research groups, contribute to team projects, and learn from classmates whose strengths differ from mine. I now regard competition as a means of pushing one another forward, and I wish to spend my undergraduate years in a place where that view is put into practice. That is the kind of environment in which I believe I will do my best work.
