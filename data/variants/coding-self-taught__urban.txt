I taught myself to program before anyone in my school could explain what programming actually entailed, and this experience has shaped the manner in which I approach learning itself. What began as mere curiosity about how the applications on my phone functioned gradually developed into a consistent habit of building small things independently; consequently, that habit altered my sense of what I am capable of achieving without awaiting permission or a printed syllabus.

The first language I chose was Python, selected solely because it was free and its tutorials were readily available. I borrowed an old laptop from my parent and installed everything by following a video guide. My first functioning program was a script that renamed a folder of study notes in a consistent format. Although it was hardly impressive, the moment it worked I understood that the computer would execute precisely what I instructed, no more and no less.

The second turning point came when I joined online forums where people posed and answered coding questions. Initially, I was hesitant to post; nevertheless, after reading discussions for a few weeks, I began answering simple queries from complete beginners. Explaining a loop or a function to someone else compelled me to scrutinise my own understanding with considerable care. Indeed, I learned more from composing those brief answers than from any single tutorial I ever watched.

The third step was developing a small web scraper to collect practice questions for the engineering entrance examination. I wanted an organised set of problems arranged by topic, and no free tool accomplished exactly what I required. It took me weeks to get it right, and I broke it on numerous occasions before it ran end to end. The final version was untidy; nevertheless, it saved me hours every week and assisted my classmate as well.

In retrospect, the most valuable aspect was not the code itself. Rather, it was learning to persist with a problem that I could not solve for days at a time. In school, I had grown accustomed to problems that possessed an established method and a printed answer at the back of the textbook. Programming, however, did not operate in that manner. I had to read documentation that I did not fully comprehend, attempt approaches that failed, and gradually determine which of my assumptions were mistaken. That patience carried over into my preparation for my final school examinations and the entrance examination; moreover, it transformed my attitude towards difficulty in general.

I wish to study computer science at the undergraduate level because I seek a structured foundation to underpin the practical skills that I developed on my own. Self-teaching gave me confidence, but it also revealed the gaps in my knowledge, particularly in mathematics and the theory underlying algorithms. A university programme would enable me to address those gaps and to work alongside teachers and classmates who share the same interest. Ultimately, I hope to apply what I learn towards building simple tools that help other students learn independently, in the manner that I did.
