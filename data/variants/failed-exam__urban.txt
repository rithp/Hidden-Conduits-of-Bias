The most valuable lesson I have learnt thus far arose from failing the engineering entrance examination on my first attempt. For a long time, I was convinced that hard work alone was sufficient, and that if I remained at my desk for long enough, the results would follow. The failure revealed to me that effort unaccompanied by honest self-assessment constitutes only half of the work, and this realisation has gradually transformed the manner in which I approach every subject I now study.

The first lesson the failure imparted was the distinction between reading and understanding. In the months preceding the examination, I had gone through my textbooks on numerous occasions. I could recall definitions and formulae, and I felt confident. However, when I sat with the paper before me, I realised that I had memorised patterns without comprehending why they worked. Consequently, questions that employed the same ideas in a slightly altered form left me stuck. After the result was declared, I began maintaining a small notebook in which I wrote each concept in my own words before proceeding further. Although this slowed me down initially, it rendered my learning more stable.

The second observation concerned the value of seeking help. Prior to the examination, I seldom approached my teacher outside of class, since I did not wish to appear weak. When I resumed my preparation, I made it a practice to bring one clearly framed question to my teacher every week. Moreover, my classmate, who was also reattempting the examination, and I began solving problems together on weekends. These modest changes made the preparation feel less isolating, and I discovered that explaining a step to my classmate frequently helped me understand it better myself.

The third lesson was learning to rest without guilt. Earlier, I would remain at my desk for many hours, yet a substantial portion of that time was spent worrying rather than working. During my second attempt at preparation, I fixed a definite stopping time each night and took one full day off every week. My concentration during study hours improved, and my parent observed that I appeared calmer at home.

In retrospect, the failure was painful; nevertheless, it compelled me to be honest with myself in a way that success never had. I ceased treating marks as the sole measure of progress and began attending to whether I genuinely understood what I was doing. I also learnt that setbacks are easier to bear when they are shared with people who care about you. These are not dramatic realisations, but they have shaped the way I now handle adversity.

I hope to carry these habits forward into my undergraduate studies. I wish to pursue engineering because I enjoy solving problems that have real consequences, and I am aware that the work will be demanding. The failure taught me that steady, honest effort matters more than any single result, and I believe this attitude will serve me well in the years ahead.
