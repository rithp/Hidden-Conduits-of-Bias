One question in physics has remained with me since an early age, and it is a simple one: why a heavy object and a light object, released at the same moment, reach the ground together. For a long time, this did not appear obvious to me. I assumed that a stone ought to fall faster than a leaf, which, in everyday life, it usually does. Accepting that my everyday intuition was mistaken, and understanding precisely why it was mistaken, is the reason I wish to pursue physics further.

The first impetus came during a Class 8 lesson. My teacher held a small coin and a flat sheet of paper in one hand and released both from the same height. The coin landed first, and the class consequently agreed that this proved the heavier object falls faster. My teacher then crumpled the paper into a tight ball and dropped both once again. On this occasion, they landed together. I found myself unable to stop reflecting on that simple alteration. The paper's mass had not changed, only its shape, and yet the outcome had reversed entirely.

The second impetus came from a chapter on Galileo. His argument was that two stones tied together could not fall faster than a single stone alone, since such reasoning would contradict itself. Moreover, Galileo constructed this case through thought alone, without the involvement of any equipment. I recall attempting to explain the argument to my parent over dinner and becoming stuck halfway through. That night, I worked through it on paper until I finally understood it.

The third impetus came considerably later, when I watched a recording of the feather and the hammer being dropped on the surface of the Moon. In an environment devoid of air, both struck the ground at precisely the same instant. What had appeared strange in a classroom demonstration now seemed almost ordinary. Air resistance had been influencing the results in the classroom; consequently, once it was removed, the physics corresponded to the theory.

In retrospect, this modest question altered the manner in which I approach problems. I learnt that physical intuition is frequently shaped by conditions we fail to notice, and that a sound theory must account for both the common case and the surprising one. Moreover, I learnt that a careful thought experiment can, at times, take one further than a hurried calculation. I ceased trusting first impressions and began asking what I might be overlooking. This habit proved valuable while I prepared for my final school examinations and for the engineering entrance examination, where the most challenging questions were usually those that penalised careless assumptions.

I now wish to study engineering at a reputable university, and I hope to devote the next four years to problems whose answers are not evident at the outset. If a single dropped coin and a sheet of paper could change how I perceive the world, I am eager to discover what a complete undergraduate programme can accomplish.
