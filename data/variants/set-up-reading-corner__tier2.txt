When I set up a small reading corner in an unused room next to my home, I did not expect it to change how I thought about learning. I had always assumed reading was a private activity. Watching a group of local children pick up books, argue about characters, and return back the next day showed me that reading can also be a shared habit, and that shared habits can be built with very little.

The idea began during a break after my final school exams itself. I had a stack of old novels, textbooks, and children's storybooks that had been sitting in cardboard boxes for years. Rather than sell them, I asked my parent if I could clear a small space in a side room. I painted one wall with a coat of leftover paint, arranged the books on two wooden shelves, and put a mat on the floor. The whole setup took about a week.

The next step was harder. I wrote a short notice on a large sheet of paper and stuck the same on the wall outside our home, saying that the room would be open on weekend afternoons. On the first weekend, no one came. On the second weekend, three children arrived, followed by two more the next hour. I had to think about how to arrange the books so that a seven year old and a fifteen year old could both find something. I sorted them by difficulty rather than by subject. Younger children could find picture books on one shelf, and older ones could look for novels on another.

One afternoon stands out. A younger reader picked up an old book of folk tales and asked me to explain a word. I was not knowing the word. Instead of pretending, I looked it up in a dictionary while the reader waited. My classmate, who had started helping me on weekends, later told me that this small moment had made the space feel serious. Children came back because they saw that the adults there were also learning.

Running the reading space taught me things I could not have learned from any single book. I learned that a shared resource needs steady effort rather than a single grand plan. I learned that people, especially young readers, notice honesty more than expertise. I also learned that I enjoy building small systems that help others, even when the results are slow and quiet. This last point only surprised me the most, because I had always thought of myself as more comfortable with problems on paper than with people.

I am applying for an undergraduate engineering programme because I want to work on systems that serve communities in practical ways. My reading space was a tiny system with real users, and it showed me the value of designing for the person who actually walks in. That habit of attention, I hope to carry it into everything I study next.
