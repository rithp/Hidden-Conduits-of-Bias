I was raised in a small town where the ordinary difficulties of daily life would repeatedly turn into questions I could not stop asking; over time, those questions became the reason I wish to pursue civil engineering. The place I come from did not possess the kind of infrastructure that residents of larger cities take for granted, and consequently, living there made me curious about how things are constructed, why some systems function as intended while others do not, and what it would ultimately take to rectify them.

The first example to which I invariably return is the water supply in my town. For most of my childhood, the taps in our house ran for only about two hours each morning, and everyone in the household had a fixed responsibility during that period. My parent filled the large storage drums, my sibling filled the smaller containers, and I was in charge of the kitchen pots. Later, as I grew older, I began reading about how municipal water systems are designed, and I gradually came to understand that our situation was not merely misfortune but rather the consequence of specific decisions regarding pipe layout, pressure planning, and maintenance.

The second example is the road just outside my school. Every year during the rainy season, that road flooded so severely that classes were frequently cancelled for two or three days. I recall standing at the edge of the water with my classmate, attempting to determine where the drain was obstructed. When I subsequently encountered a proper stormwater drainage diagram in a textbook, the matter became clear to me. The problem was not the rain; rather, it was that nobody had designed for it.

The third example is smaller; nevertheless, it has remained with me. My teacher once brought a broken solar lantern to class and asked us to deduce why it had stopped working. We spent almost an hour examining it before we discovered a loose wire. That single afternoon made me realise that most things around me were not mysterious; they had been designed by someone and, therefore, could be understood, and often repaired, by someone else.

In retrospect, I believe that what my town gave me was not a set of grievances but a habit of observation. I learned to regard a leaking pipe or a flooded road as a question rather than as a fact of life. Moreover, conversations at home about the water bill, or with my parent about why repairs to the road never lasted, have shaped the manner in which I now read news reports on public projects. I doubt that I would possess this way of viewing the world had I grown up somewhere the systems already functioned.

It is for this reason that I wish to study civil engineering at the undergraduate level. Following my final school examinations and the entrance examination, I hope to work on public infrastructure for smaller towns and cities like the one I came from, where good design matters most and yet often arrives last.
