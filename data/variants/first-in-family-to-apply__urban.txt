I am the first member of my family to apply to a university. Although this fact remained quietly in the background throughout my school years, it was only in the last two years that I began to recognise the extent to which it had shaped my approach to studying, the questions I asked, and how I envisioned my future. Applying to a university was not a decision made over dinner; rather, it was a gradual process of understanding what a university was, what it could offer, and whether it was intended for someone like me.

My parent works long hours in a small local business and did not complete school. The books at home were limited to my school textbooks and a few old notebooks. When I informed my parent of my intention to prepare for the engineering entrance examination, the response was supportive, albeit cautious. My parent had no map for what I was about to undertake. Consequently, decisions that I now recognise as routine, such as selecting which subjects to prioritise or determining when to purchase practice materials, felt rather onerous. I therefore learned to make these choices with care, and to explain them at home in simple terms.

In my final year of school, my teacher observed that I remained after class to enquire about problems I had not understood, and subsequently began leaving additional worksheets on my desk. I recall one afternoon on which my teacher explained a physics problem twice, then a third time, before finally drawing a small diagram in the corner of my notebook. That diagram is still there. It taught me that asking again is not a sign of weakness, and that most learning occurs after the first attempt has failed.

A third turning point came when I began researching universities independently. During the lunch break, I sat in the school computer room and read course descriptions that I did not fully comprehend. I noted down unfamiliar words and looked them up later. Moreover, I asked a classmate whose older sibling was in college to explain how the application process worked. Gradually, a picture took shape; although it was not complete, it was my own.

Being first is not merely a story of hardship; it is a story of building a small internal map from scratch. I have frequently been uncertain whether my questions were the right ones, or whether my plans were realistic. Nevertheless, I have learned that uncertainty is not a signal to stop. Rather, it is a signal to gather further information, to consult more people, and to record what I genuinely want, as opposed to what I believe I ought to want.

I wish to study engineering because I enjoy the process of taking a large, ill-defined problem and decomposing it into smaller, solvable parts. At a university, I hope to work alongside people who ask more difficult questions than I presently know how to ask, and gradually to become the kind of student who is capable of answering them.
