The most useful lesson I have learned so far came from failing the engineering entrance exam on my first attempt. For a long time I was believing that hard work by itself was enough, and that if I sat at my desk long enough the results would follow. The failure showed me that effort without honest self-assessment is only half of the work, and this idea has slowly changed how I approach every subject I now study.

The first thing the failure taught me was the difference between reading and understanding. In the months before the exam I had gone through my textbooks many many times. I could recall definitions and formulas, and I felt confident. But when I sat with the paper in front of me, I realised that I had memorised patterns without knowing why they worked. Questions that used the same ideas in a slightly different form left me stuck. After the result, I started keeping a small notebook where I wrote each concept in my own words before moving on. This slowed me down at first, but it made my learning more stable.

The second thing I noticed was the value of asking for help. Before the exam I rarely spoke to my teacher outside of class, because I did not want to appear weak. When I began preparing again, I made it a habit to bring one clear question to my teacher each week. My classmate who was also reattempting the exam and I began solving problems together on weekends. These small changes made preparation feel less lonely, and I found that explaining a step to my classmate often helped me understand the same better myself.

The third thing was learning to rest without guilt. Earlier, I would sit at my desk for many hours, but a large part of that time was spent worrying rather than working. During my second preparation, I have fixed a clear stopping time each night and taken one full day off every week. My focus during study hours improved, and my parent noticed that I seemed calmer at home.

Looking back, the failure was painful, but it forced me to be honest with myself in a way that success never had. Marks, I stopped treating them as the only measure of progress and began paying attention to whether I actually understood what I was doing. I also learned that setbacks are easier to carry when they are shared with people who care about you. These are not dramatic realisations, but they have shaped how I now handle difficulty.

I hope to carry these habits into my undergraduate studies. I want to study engineering because I enjoy solving problems that have real consequences, and I know that the work will be demanding. The failure taught me that steady, honest effort matters more than a single result, and I believe the same will serve me well in the years ahead.
