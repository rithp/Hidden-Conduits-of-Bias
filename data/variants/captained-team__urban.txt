Serving as captain of my school football team taught me that leading a struggling group differs considerably from leading a successful one. I assumed the captaincy at the beginning of an important year, anticipating an ordinary season. Instead, we lost our first four matches, our best striker fractured a leg, and two senior players withdrew from the team in order to concentrate on their preparation for the engineering entrance examination. By the middle of the season, I had realised that my responsibility was not to inspire from the front but to keep the team united while everyone adapted to new roles.

The first change I had to make concerned our practice sessions. Previously, these sessions followed a fixed plan that the coach had employed for years. However, with a smaller squad and less experience, that plan was no longer suited to us. Consequently, I began meeting our coach every Monday morning before classes in order to redesign the week. We shortened drills, introduced more small-sided games, and allowed younger players more time on the ball. Although the change was uncomfortable initially, it gradually built confidence in those players who had remained on the bench for a long time.

The second change concerned communication. I observed that after every defeat, the team would fall silent, and minor mistakes would escalate into arguments. I therefore began conducting a brief meeting after each match, in which every player was required to mention one aspect that had gone well and one that needed improvement. It felt awkward during the first few weeks, and some players resisted it. Nevertheless, over time, these meetings made it easier for us to speak honestly without assigning blame, and our defensive errors declined noticeably in the second half of the season.

The third change related to how I managed my own performance. As captain, I had always believed that I needed to be the best player on the field. During that difficult season, however, I began playing a deeper role so that a younger midfielder could assume greater attacking responsibility. Although my personal statistics declined, the team started scoring more consistently. Accepting a smaller individual role proved to be one of the harder lessons of the year.

In retrospect, the season taught me that leadership is often quiet work. It is easy to lead when results are favourable, since the group sustains itself. It is considerably harder when confidence is low, when injuries alter the plan every week, and when the people around me are also preparing for their final school examinations. What I learned was that clear routines, honest conversation and personal flexibility matter more than any single motivational speech. Moreover, I learned that I need not always be the loudest person in the room in order to make a difference.

I wish to study engineering because I enjoy solving problems that involve both technical reasoning and people. The season demonstrated to me that structured, patient effort can rebuild something that appears broken. I hope to bring the same approach to my studies and to the teams I will work with at university.
