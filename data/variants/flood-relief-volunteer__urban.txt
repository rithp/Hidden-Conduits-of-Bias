When our town was flooded last year, I spent two weeks assisting at a relief camp near my school. Prior to that, I had regarded civic responsibility as something one merely reads about in textbooks. The flood, however, demonstrated to me that it is a practical set of tasks that ordinary people carry out collectively.

The rains commenced on a Tuesday and continued without pause for four days. By Friday, water had entered the low-lying colonies surrounding my school. A local committee opened the school building as a relief camp, and my parent suggested that I go and enquire whether I could be of help. The teacher in charge assigned me to the food line. My responsibility was to verify the count of packets arriving from a nearby kitchen, record it in a register, and pass the packets on to two volunteers who distributed them to families. Although it sounds simple, it demanded considerable attention. On the first evening, I made a counting error that left one row of people without dinner for almost an hour. Consequently, I learned to slow down and read the numbers aloud before writing them down.

The second task I undertook was cleaning. Once the water began to recede, some families returned to houses full of mud. I accompanied a group of college students to a small house near the market, and we carried buckets, brooms, and a hand pump. My classmate and I worked on the kitchen floor, where the tiles were coated in a brown layer that would not come off with plain water. We therefore had to scrape it with a piece of wood before mopping. Cleaning that one small room took the entire morning. Before we left, the owner of the house made us tea and asked our names in a quiet voice.

The third area in which I assisted was the register at the camp. Many families had lost their identity documents in the flood. A senior teacher was helping people fill in a simple form so that the local office could issue them temporary papers. I sat beside that teacher and entered the details for those who could not write easily, a task I continued for two days.

On reflection, I believe the flood taught me something that no classroom assignment had: that most useful work is repetitive and slow. Nobody at the camp did anything heroic. People counted packets, cleaned floors, and filled in forms. Although the scale of the problem was large, the actions were small, and each of them had to be done well. Moreover, I observed how quickly a group of strangers can organise itself when the situation demands it.

It is because of this experience that I wish to study civil engineering. Flood management, drainage design, and disaster planning are technical problems; nevertheless, they are also human ones. I hope that during my undergraduate years I can learn both dimensions, and that I can return to work of this kind with better skills to offer.
