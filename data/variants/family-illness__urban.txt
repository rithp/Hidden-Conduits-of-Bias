During my final year of school, when my parent fell seriously ill, I learnt that studying involves not merely managing one's hours but also protecting a small space in one's mind for the work, even when everything around one feels uncertain. That year taught me more about focus and responsibility than any amount of examination preparation could have.

The illness was diagnosed a few months prior to my final school examinations. The treatment necessitated regular hospital visits, and a family member was required to be present on each occasion. Since my sibling was younger and my other parent had to continue working, I undertook a large number of these visits. School was still in session, and the engineering entrance examination was merely a few months away. Initially, I believed that I would simply lose the year. Gradually, however, I discovered ways to persevere.

The first was studying in the hospital waiting room. I began carrying a small folder of worked-out problems along with a notebook for revision. Since the waiting rooms were noisy, I was unable to read any new material there; nevertheless, I could revise what I had already learnt. Over the course of several weeks, this quiet revision time accumulated into something meaningful. Moreover, it gave me a means of feeling useful during long and uncertain hours.

The second was the assistance of a classmate who lived nearby. My classmate began sharing the day's notes with me, together with photographs of the board work I had missed. This assistance was not dramatic, but it ensured that I did not fall behind on new topics in physics and mathematics. Through this, I learnt that asking for help is not a sign of weakness; it is simply a way of getting the work done.

The third was a teacher who permitted me to reschedule a monthly test. My teacher did not make a public matter of it, remarking only that health comes first and that I should inform the office whenever I was ready. That small act of kindness lessened my fear that a single missed test would determine my future.

In retrospect, the most difficult lesson was accepting that I could not do everything simultaneously. I had to relinquish certain things, including social plans and a few additional practice papers that I had hoped to complete. I also had to accept help without feeling obliged to offer anyone an explanation. Although this was uncomfortable at first, I gradually came to recognise that steady, honest effort matters more than attempting to appear untroubled. My parent recovered slowly, and I concluded the year with results which, while not my best, were far better than I had feared.

I now aspire to study engineering, as I wish to work on problems that have practical consequences for people. The months I spent in hospital corridors demonstrated to me that clear thinking and careful preparation can be of help even in difficult circumstances. Consequently, I intend to bring the same steady attention to my studies at university and, eventually, to a career in which I can be of use to others.
