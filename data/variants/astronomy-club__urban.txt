The night I first observed Saturn through a small telescope on our school terrace, I realised that curiosity does not require expensive equipment or a special background in order to be genuine. Establishing an astronomy group at my school was the direct result of that realisation, and it has since shaped how I think about learning, community and my own future.

When I proposed the idea to my teacher, we had no budget and only two working telescopes, which had been borrowed from a science laboratory. I drew up a rough plan for weekly meetings, printed simple flyers and put them up near the noticeboard. Six students attended the first session. I had prepared a short talk on the phases of the moon, and I recall feeling nervous, since I was uncertain whether anyone would find it interesting. Nevertheless, by the end of the term, twenty students were attending regularly.

For our first public event, we organised a viewing of a partial lunar eclipse on the school ground. My classmate and I spent two weekends verifying the timings, drawing simple diagrams and preparing short explanations for the younger students who wished to attend. Nearly a hundred people came, including several parents. While some of the questions were simple, others proved harder than I had anticipated. Answering them honestly, and acknowledging whenever I did not know something, taught me more than any prepared talk.

The group very nearly stopped meeting during the months preceding my final school examinations. Attendance declined, and I felt pressure to concentrate solely on my own preparation. Rather than closing the group, however, I requested two younger members to conduct shorter sessions. They handled this capably, and consequently the group continued without me for a full term. Witnessing something I had initiated grow beyond my direct involvement proved both harder and more satisfying than I had expected.

In retrospect, I recognise that the astronomy group taught me lessons which classroom study alone could not. I learnt that a small idea can build a community, provided it is offered patiently and without excessive ego. I learnt that admitting ignorance is often more useful than pretending to know. Moreover, I learnt that leadership is quieter than I had once believed; it is less about being at the front and more about creating space for others to step forward. I had heard these ideas before, yet understanding them through my own experience was different. I also learnt that a project I care about need not be dramatic in order to be meaningful. Consistency mattered more than any single moment I could point to.

As I prepare for the engineering entrance examination and consider the years ahead, I wish to continue working at the meeting point of curiosity and structure. I hope to pursue a field that allows me to build things, scrutinise them carefully and share what I learn. Astronomy may or may not remain my principal interest; nevertheless, the habits it gave me will.
