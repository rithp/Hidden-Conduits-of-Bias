For the past three years, I have devoted the greater part of my school holidays to assisting at my family's small shop. When I began, I presumed the work would be straightforward, since the shop is modest in size and its tasks appeared routine. Over time, however, I realised that a small business is essentially a long sequence of minor problems, and that learning to address them methodically is a skill in its own right. This is the principal lesson I have drawn from those hours behind the counter.

The first lesson concerned the management of stock. Initially, I noticed items only once we had run out of them, which consequently cost us sales. My parent asked me to maintain a simple notebook recording what was sold each day. Within a few weeks, a pattern had become apparent: certain items sold considerably faster at weekends, whereas others sold better in the mornings. This prompted me to plan orders with greater care, and the shop began to run short of stock less frequently.

The second lesson concerned dealing with a difficult customer. One afternoon, a customer returned an item, claiming it was damaged, although it had been used for several days. My first instinct was to argue. My parent, however, intervened, listened calmly and offered a modest exchange instead. The customer left satisfied and returned the following week. I understood then that retaining a customer is frequently worth more than winning a single argument.

The third lesson was the importance of verifying figures. At the end of one month, our totals failed to match the cash in the drawer. I sat down with the notebook and the receipts, and eventually traced the discrepancy to a single entry that had been recorded twice. Although the error was small, it had caused confusion for many days. Consequently, I began to check the totals every evening rather than waiting until the end of the month.

In retrospect, none of these lessons was dramatic. They were gradual and modest, and they arose from repeating the same tasks many times. What surprised me, nevertheless, was the extent to which this steady work altered my approach to my studies. I now divide large tasks into smaller steps, keep brief notes as I work, and verify my answers before proceeding. Moreover, I have grown more patient with problems that require time to resolve. These habits have served me well during my final school examinations and in my preparation for the engineering entrance examination.

I wish to study engineering because I enjoy taking a large system apart and understanding how each component supports the whole. The shop taught me that systems function only when their individual components are monitored carefully. I hope to bring the same patience to my studies at university and to the projects I undertake in the years ahead.
