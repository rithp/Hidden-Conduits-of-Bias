For the past three years, I have spent most of my school holidays helping at my family's small shop. When I began, I thought the work would be simple, since the shop is small and the tasks looked routine. Over time I realised that a small business is really a long chain of small small problems, and that learning to handle these problems in order is a skill in itself. This is the main thing I have taken away from those hours behind the counter.

The first thing I learned was how to keep track of stock. In the beginning, I only noticed items when we ran out of them, which meant we lost sales. My parent has asked me to keep a simple notebook, writing down what was sold each day. After a few weeks, I could see that some items sold much faster on weekends, and others sold better in the mornings. This made me plan orders in a more careful way, and the shop began to run out of items less often.

The second thing I learned was how to speak to a difficult customer. One afternoon, a customer returned back an item and said it was damaged, though it had been used for several days. My first response was to argue. My parent stepped in, listened calmly, and offered a small exchange instead. The customer left satisfied and returned the next week. I understood then that keeping a customer is often worth more than winning a single argument.

The third thing I learned was the value of checking numbers. At the end of one month, our totals did not match the cash in the drawer. I sat down with the notebook and receipts and traced the mistake to a single entry that had been written twice. It was a small error, but the same had caused confusion for many days. After that, I began to check totals every evening rather than waiting for the end of the month.

Looking back, none of these lessons were dramatic. They were slow and small, and they came from repeating the same tasks many times. What surprised me was how much this steady work changed the way I approach my studies. I now break large tasks into smaller steps, keep short notes as I work, and check my answers before moving on. Problems that take time to solve, I have also become more patient with them. These habits have helped me during my final school exams and while preparing for the engineering entrance exam.

I want to study engineering because I am enjoying taking a large system apart and understanding how each small piece supports the whole. The shop taught me that systems only work when the small pieces are watched carefully. I hope to bring the same patience to my studies at university, and to the projects I will take up in the years ahead.
