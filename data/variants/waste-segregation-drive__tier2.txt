The most useful thing I have learned outside a classroom is that a small, steady effort can slowly change how a neighbourhood behaves. I understood this only after spending nearly a year running a waste segregation drive in the lane where I live. What began as a school assignment, it turned into a project I could not put down, and it taught me more about people than any textbook has.

The first concrete step was preparing a simple chart that showed which items counted as wet waste, dry waste, and hazardous waste. I printed twenty copies at a local shop and walked from door to door in my lane. Many neighbours were polite but distracted. A few asked useful questions about wet kitchen waste and used batteries. I noted their questions and updated the same the next week. This slow back and forth taught me that clear information matters less than patient repetition.

The second effort involved the person who collected our household waste. I asked if a two bucket system would help, and my parent agreed to buy two colour coded bins for our home first. Within a month itself, three other homes on the lane did the same. The waste collector began to sort a little faster because fewer bags were mixed. It was a small change, but I could see the effect at the end of every week when the pile at the corner shrank.

The third effort was through a group I formed at my school. Five classmates joined me, and we spent two Saturdays cleaning a small park near the lane. We collected plastic wrappers, glass pieces, old cloth and all. My teacher helped us arrange a dustbin near the park entrance. The park stayed cleaner for several weeks, though not forever. I learned that any physical clean up needs a system behind it, or the same rubbish returns.

Working on this project changed how I think about ordinary problems. I used to believe that civic issues needed large campaigns and famous names to move at all. Now I am believing that most useful change is quiet, slow, and repetitive, and it depends on the same people showing up week after week. I also learned to accept partial success. Not every neighbour segregated waste, and the park did not stay spotless. Even so, the average behaviour on my lane shifted in a small but visible way, and that felt worth the time.

This experience has shaped what I want to study next. I hope to pursue an undergraduate degree in engineering, with a strong interest in environmental systems and public infrastructure. I want to understand about how cities design waste flows, how data can guide better collection, and how simple design choices can push people towards better habits. My local drive taught me that engineering is not only about building new things. It is also about arranging existing things so that they work a little better each day.
