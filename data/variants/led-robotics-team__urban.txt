Leading a small robotics team during my final year of school taught me that effective leadership depends less on issuing instructions and more on listening to the people around me. When my teacher asked me to head a four-member team building a robot for the school science fair, I initially assumed that the role would be straightforward. I would plan the work, allocate the tasks, and monitor the progress. Within two weeks, however, I realised that the work would not advance unless every member felt heard.

The first instance that altered my thinking occurred during the design phase. Two of my teammates wanted the robot to follow a black line using infrared sensors, whereas one preferred a camera-based approach. I had already concluded that infrared would be simpler, and I stated as much at the beginning of the meeting. Consequently, the teammate who had proposed the camera stopped contributing for the remainder of the week. When I eventually asked what the matter was, my classmate replied that no one had listened to the idea. I therefore convened another meeting, in which we compared the two methods on paper, and the group chose the infrared design together. The mood changed at once.

The second instance concerned sensor calibration. The robot repeatedly drifted off the line, and we were unable to identify the cause. Rather than attempting to resolve the issue myself, I divided the problem into three parts and assigned each teammate one section to test. Within two days, a teammate discovered that surface reflection was confusing the sensor at one particular point on the track. A small piece of matte tape resolved the problem. I would not have arrived at the answer on my own.

The third instance related to time management. Every member of the team was also preparing for the engineering entrance examination. Accordingly, I devised a schedule that respected everyone's study hours, and I kept our meetings brief and focused. When one teammate had a difficult week, another member offered to take over the coding work. The project remained on track because the team supported one another, not because I pressed anyone.

In retrospect, I observe that my instinct at the outset was to control the outcome, and that my most useful moments arrived when I ceased doing so. The project taught me that leadership is a practice of trust. I had to trust that my teammates would put forward good ideas, that they would seek help when necessary, and that the work would improve if I stepped back. This shift was uncomfortable, and I continue to find it difficult in group settings; nevertheless, I now recognise it as a skill that I wish to keep developing.

I wish to study engineering because I enjoy the process of transforming an idea into a working system, and I want to undertake that work alongside other people. A university programme with team-based projects and open laboratories will enable me to practise both the technical and the human dimensions of engineering that this small project began to teach me.
