In my final year of school, I was entrusted with leading the organising committee for our annual cultural day. The event did take place; however, many aspects of it did not proceed as planned. In retrospect, the most valuable lesson I learnt was that planning on paper differs considerably from planning for real people, and that a leader is judged less by the plan itself and more by how they respond when it fails.

The first difficulty concerned the schedule. I had prepared a timetable which assumed that every performance would commence punctually. In practice, the first dance group arrived late because their bus had been delayed. Consequently, the entire morning was pushed back by almost an hour. I had not incorporated any buffer time into the schedule, nor had I devised a contingency plan for the opening slot. My teacher therefore had to intervene and rearrange the order of events on the spot. It was then that I realised a schedule is useful only if it can absorb minor failures.

The second problem related to communication. I had circulated instructions to the various teams over a group chat, but I had not confirmed that everyone had read them. On the day of the event, two teams arrived at the wrong venue within the school, since I had altered the room allocation late in the week and had announced the change only verbally. I spent almost thirty minutes hurrying between blocks to redirect them. This experience taught me that important updates must be reiterated in writing, and that silence in a group chat does not signify agreement.

The third issue pertained to budgeting. I had estimated the cost of decorations and refreshments on the basis of the previous year's figures, without verifying current prices. When the volunteers returned from the market, the bill was considerably higher than the amount I had planned for. I was compelled to seek my teacher's approval to draw upon a small reserve fund. This was embarrassing, as it made the committee appear careless, even though the error was mine alone.

After the event, I spent a considerable time reflecting on why these problems seemed so avoidable in hindsight. The pattern was evident. In each instance, I had assumed that the ideal version of the plan would materialise, and I had not considered carefully what might go wrong. I had mistaken effort for preparation. Genuine preparation, I now understand, entails recording the things that could fail and deciding in advance how to address them.

This experience is one of the reasons I wish to study engineering. Engineering concerns not merely the design of systems that function, but the design of systems that continue to function when a component fails. I wish to acquire the tools that enable people to reason clearly about failure, and I hope that a rigorous undergraduate programme will equip me with both the technical knowledge and the habits of mind required to do so well.
