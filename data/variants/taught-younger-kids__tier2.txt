When I began tutoring younger students two years ago, I thought I was doing them a favour. I am now believing the opposite is closer to the truth. Teaching others has taught me more about how learning actually works than any class I have ever sat through, and it has quietly reshaped how I approach my own studies.

The first student I tutored was a neighbour, three years younger than me, who had almost given up on mathematics. My parent suggested I try to help, mostly because tuition was expensive and the family lived on our street. In the first few sessions, I have explained problems the way my teacher explained them, and the student stared blankly at the page. It was only after I asked what the student had understood from a question that I realised the confusion started much earlier than I had assumed. We went back to fractions, then to place value, and then slowly forward again. By the end of the term, the marks had improved, but what surprised me more was that the student was asking questions without being prompted.

The second experience was a small group of four students preparing for their final school exams. I taught them physics for an hour twice a week. Each of them was stuck in different different places, and I could not use one explanation for all of them. I began keeping a small notebook where I wrote down which idea each student was missing. Some needed diagrams, some wanted numerical practice, and one only understood when I used an example from cricket.

The third experience was informal. A classmate asked me to explain probability the night before an internal test. I had ninety minutes only. I discovered that when I had no time to prepare, I fell back on the parts of the topic I understood deeply and skipped the parts I only half knew. That was an uncomfortable but useful discovery about my own preparation.

Looking back, tutoring has changed how I study. I no longer measure understanding by whether I can solve a problem once. I measure it by whether I can explain the steps to someone who has never seen the topic. When I cannot, I know where my own gaps are. I have also become more patient with myself, because I have watched students I tutored move from confusion to clarity, and the process, I know it is slower than it looks from the outside. I used to feel embarrassed about asking questions in class, and now I ask them more freely.

As I prepare for the entrance exam and for undergraduate study in engineering, I am wanting to keep this habit. I hope to join a college where students help each other openly, and where I can continue to learn by teaching. I believe the best engineers are also good explainers, and I would like to be both.
