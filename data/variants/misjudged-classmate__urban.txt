During my final year of school, I formed an erroneous opinion of a classmate, and the process of correcting it taught me more than most of my classes did. Within a few weeks, I had concluded that this classmate was indifferent towards studies. That judgement was hasty and comfortable; nevertheless, as I subsequently learnt, it was entirely mistaken.

The first indication that I was mistaken arose during a group assignment in physics. Our teacher instructed us to form pairs, and I was partnered with this classmate purely by chance. I anticipated that I would have to do most of the work. Instead, my classmate arrived with a notebook full of solved problems, the majority of which were more difficult than those that had been assigned. When I enquired about their source, I was told that a public library near my classmate's house possessed a few old textbooks, and that my classmate had been working through them for months. I had presumed indifference because my classmate remained quiet in class; however, that quietness had no connection whatsoever with a lack of interest.

The second indication emerged after a mid-term examination. I observed that my classmate had scored higher than I had in mathematics, even though I had spent hours attending a paid tutorial and my classmate had not. When I asked how this was possible, my classmate merely remarked that the tutorial notes I had been displaying so proudly were not substantially different from what any attentive reader could derive from the textbook. The remark was made without any trace of sharpness, simply as a statement of fact; consequently, it compelled me to consider how much of my confidence had stemmed from my access to assistance.

The third indication came during the months preceding the engineering entrance examination. We began meeting after school to revise, since my classmate could not afford the coaching that many of my other friends attended. What surprised me was the patience my classmate showed towards my mistakes. I was frequently careless; nevertheless, rather than pointing this out with irritation, my classmate would work through the problem again from the beginning. My parent noticed the change in my study habits and asked what had brought it about. At the time, I did not have a clear answer.

In retrospect, I recognise that my initial judgement rested on factors that had no bearing on the classmate actually before me. I had mistaken quietness for disinterest, and a lack of resources for a lack of ability. My view was altered not by any single dramatic moment but by a gradual accumulation of evidence that I had been misreading the signals. Moreover, I began to perceive how readily I had accepted a narrative about my classmate that suited my own sense of standing in the class.

I wish to pursue my university studies with this lesson in mind. I hope to undertake courses in engineering and, alongside them, subjects that examine how people think and how systems can be made fair. The classmate whom I once dismissed enabled me to understand that careful attention to people is a habit worth cultivating, and I intend to continue cultivating it.
