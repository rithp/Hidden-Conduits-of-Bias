When I established a small reading corner in an unused room adjoining my home, I did not anticipate that it would alter how I thought about learning. I had always assumed that reading was a private activity. Nevertheless, watching a group of local children pick up books, debate characters, and return the following day showed me that reading can also be a shared habit, and that shared habits can be cultivated with very little.

The idea originated during a break after my final school examinations. I had a stack of old novels, textbooks, and children's storybooks that had been lying in cardboard boxes for years. Rather than sell them, I asked my parent whether I could clear a small space in a side room. I painted one wall with a coat of leftover paint, arranged the books on two wooden shelves, and placed a mat on the floor. The entire setup took approximately a week.

The subsequent step proved more difficult. I wrote a brief notice on a large sheet of paper and affixed it to the wall outside our home, stating that the room would be open on weekend afternoons. On the first weekend, nobody came. On the second weekend, however, three children arrived, followed by two more within the next hour. Consequently, I had to consider how to arrange the books so that a seven year old and a fifteen year old could both find something suitable. I sorted them by difficulty rather than by subject. Younger children could locate picture books on one shelf, while older ones could look for novels on another.

One afternoon remains particularly memorable. A younger reader picked up an old book of folk tales and asked me to explain a word. I did not know the word. Instead of pretending otherwise, I looked it up in a dictionary while the reader waited. My classmate, who had begun assisting me on weekends, later told me that this small moment had made the space feel serious. Children returned because they observed that the adults there were also learning.

Running the reading space taught me lessons I could not have acquired from any single book. I learnt that a shared resource requires steady effort rather than a single grand plan. I learnt, moreover, that people, especially young readers, notice honesty more than expertise. I also learnt that I enjoy building small systems that assist others, even when the results are slow and quiet. This final realisation surprised me the most, since I had always regarded myself as more comfortable with problems on paper than with people.

I am applying for an undergraduate engineering programme because I wish to work on systems that serve communities in practical ways. My reading space was a modest system with real users, and it demonstrated the value of designing for the person who actually walks in. I hope to carry that habit of attentiveness into everything I study next.
