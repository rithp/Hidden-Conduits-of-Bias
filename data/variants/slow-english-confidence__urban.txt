For a considerable period, I believed that remaining quiet in class was the safest course of action available to me. If I did not speak, I could not be wrong, and I could not be laughed at. Reflecting upon my school years, I now recognise that this belief cost me more than it protected me. Learning to speak up has, in fact, been the most useful change I have made in myself.

During my early years at school, I sat near the back and answered only when the teacher addressed me by name. Whenever a question was posed to the entire class, I would lower my eyes to my notebook, even when I knew the answer. On one occasion, my teacher asked who could explain a maths problem, and I remained silent while a classmate offered an answer that closely resembled what I had written down. I experienced a peculiar mixture of relief and disappointment, which stayed with me longer than I had anticipated.

The first shift occurred during a group project in middle school. My group was required to deliver a short presentation, and nobody was willing to speak. I volunteered to handle one section, largely because the silence within the group had become uncomfortable. When my turn arrived, my voice was thin and my hands were cold; nevertheless, I finished. My teacher offered a brief, honest remark that my explanation had been clear. Although the comment was ordinary, it marked the first occasion on which I associated speaking with something other than fear.

By senior school, I had established a rule for myself: at least one question or comment in every class, even if it was a simple one. On certain days, I failed. On others, I asked something that prompted a longer discussion, and I observed that the classmate beside me had wished to ask the very same question. Gradually, I came to understand that my silence had not been a private matter. It had removed a voice from the room, one that others might have found useful.

The change was slow and frequently felt unnatural. I did not become a confident speaker, and I am still not one today. What I learned instead was that confidence is not a fixed trait; rather, it develops from small acts, repeated. The fear of being wrong in public had rendered me a passive learner. Once I began to speak, however, I began to think more carefully, since I was obliged to defend my reasoning before others. This has proved more valuable to me than any single lesson from a textbook.

As I prepare to commence my undergraduate studies in engineering, I intend to carry this lesson forward. University classrooms and project teams will demand more than quiet effort. They will require me to argue, to question, and occasionally to be wrong in the presence of others. I remain uncomfortable with that prospect, but I am no longer avoiding it. I hope that my time at university allows me to continue this slow work of finding my voice.
