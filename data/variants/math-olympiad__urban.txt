Preparing for a mathematics competition in my final year of school taught me that sustained effort matters more than innate talent. Before I resolved to attempt the competition, I was convinced that those who excelled in mathematics were simply built differently. Six months of preparation, however, transformed both my understanding of the subject and my understanding of my own work habits.

The first lesson I learned concerned the value of a fixed daily routine. I set aside two hours every evening for problems, and I maintained a notebook in which I wrote out solutions in full. Initially, I could solve only two or three problems in a session; by the end of the third month, however, I could complete a full problem set on number theory within the same time. Although the routine felt tedious at first, it established a foundation on which I could rely as the problems grew more difficult.

The second lesson concerned how to interpret my own mistakes. My teacher suggested that I annotate every incorrect answer with a brief note on what had gone wrong, whether it was a careless arithmetic slip, a missing case, or an incorrect assumption. Reviewing those notes at the end of each week revealed a pattern. I was repeatedly losing marks on the same kind of error; consequently, the notes compelled me to slow down at that particular step in subsequent problems.

The third lesson concerned the value of collaborating with a classmate. Once a week, my classmate and I met at the school library and worked through problems together on a whiteboard. Explaining a step aloud proved more demanding than writing it down, and my classmate often asked a question that revealed a gap in my reasoning. This altered the way I approached independent practice, since I began to ask myself the same kind of questions.

The competition itself did not unfold as I had hoped. I solved fewer problems than I had anticipated, and I made a careless error on a question I had practised many times. For a few days afterwards, I felt that the entire effort had been wasted. Nevertheless, in retrospect, I no longer hold that view. The habits I developed over those six months carried over into the manner in which I studied for my final school examinations and the engineering entrance examination. Moreover, I learned that a single result does not define what I have gained, and that a prolonged period of quiet work is often more valuable than one high-pressure test.

I intend to continue studying mathematics as part of my engineering degree, and I hope to work on problems that combine mathematical reasoning with computer science. The competition taught me that I am capable of confronting a demanding subject, provided I am willing to build my understanding one small step at a time. I am therefore prepared to carry that approach into university.
