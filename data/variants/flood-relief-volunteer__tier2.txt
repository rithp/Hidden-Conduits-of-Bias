When our town flooded last year, I spent two weeks helping at a relief camp near my school. Before that, I had thought of civic responsibility as something you read about in textbooks only. The flood showed me that it is a practical set of tasks that ordinary people carry out together.

The rains started on a Tuesday and did not stop for four days. By Friday itself, water had entered the low-lying colonies around my school. A local committee opened the school building as a relief camp, and my parent suggested I go and ask if I could help. The teacher in charge put me on the food line. My job was to check the count of packets that arrived from a nearby kitchen, note it in a register, and pass the packets to two volunteers who handed them to families. It sounds simple, but it required attention. On the first evening, I made a counting error that left one row of people without dinner for almost an hour. After that I learned to slow down and read the numbers aloud before writing them.

The second task I took on was cleaning. Once the water began to recede, some families returned to houses full of mud. I went with a group of college students to a small house near the market. We carried buckets, brooms, and a hand pump. The kitchen floor, my classmate and I worked on it. The tiles were coated in a brown layer that would not come off with plain water. We had to scrape it with a piece of wood before mopping. It took the whole morning to clean one small room. The owner of the house made us tea before we left and asked our names in a quiet voice.

The third thing I helped with was the register at the camp. Many families have lost their identity documents in the flood. A senior teacher was helping people fill in a simple form so that the local office could give them temporary papers. I sat next to that teacher and wrote in the details for people who could not write easily. I did this for two days.

Looking back, I think the flood taught me something that no classroom assignment had. It taught me that most useful work is repetitive and slow. Nobody at the camp did anything heroic. People counted packets, cleaned floors, filled forms and all. The scale of the problem was large, but the actions were small and had to be done well. I also saw how quickly a group of strangers can organise itself when the situation demands it.

I want to study civil engineering because of this experience. Flood management, drainage design, and disaster planning are technical problems, but they are also human ones. I hope that during my undergraduate years I can learn both sides, and that I can return back to work of this kind with better skills to offer.
