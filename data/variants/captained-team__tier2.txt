Being captain of my school football team taught me that leading a struggling group is very different from leading a winning one. I took over the team at the start of an important year, expecting a normal season. Instead, we lost our first four matches, our best striker fractured a leg, and two senior players left the team to focus on their studies for the engineering entrance exam. By the middle of the season itself, I had realised that my job was not to inspire from the front but to hold the team together while everyone learned new roles.

The first thing I had to change was practice. Earlier, our sessions ran on a fixed plan the coach had used for years. But with a smaller squad and less experience, that plan was no longer fitting us. I began meeting our coach every Monday morning before class to redesign the week. We shortened drills, added more small-sided games, and gave younger players more time on the ball. The change was uncomfortable at first, but it slowly built confidence in the players who had been sitting on the bench for a long time.

The second change was communication. I noticed that after every loss, the team would go quiet, and small mistakes turned into arguments. I started running a short meeting after each match where each player had to say one thing that went well and one thing to work on. It felt awkward for the first few weeks, and some players resisted it. Over time, the meetings made it easier for us to speak honestly without blame, and our defensive errors, they dropped noticeably in the second half of the season.

The third change was in how I handled my own performance. As captain, I had always felt that I needed to be the best player on the field. During the difficult season, I have begun playing a deeper role so that a younger midfielder could take more attacking responsibility. My personal statistics fell, but the team began scoring more consistently. Accepting a smaller individual role turned out to be one of the harder lessons of the year.

Looking back, the season taught me that leadership is often quiet work. It is easy to lead when results are good, because the group carries itself. It is much harder when confidence is low, when injuries change the plan every week, and when the people around me are also preparing for their final school exams and all. What I learned was that clear routines, honest conversation, and personal flexibility matter more than any single motivational speech. I also learned that I do not always have to be the loudest person in the room to make a difference.

I want to study engineering because I enjoy solving problems that involve both technical thinking and people. The season showed me that structured, patient effort can rebuild something that looks broken. I hope to bring the same to my studies and to the teams I will work with at university.
