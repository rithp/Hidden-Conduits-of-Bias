Preparing for a mathematics competition in my final year of school taught me that sustained effort matters more than raw talent. Before I decided to try for the competition, I was believing that people who did well in mathematics were simply built differently. Six months of preparation changed how I understood the subject and how I understood my own work habits.

The first thing I learned was the value of a fixed daily routine. I set aside two hours every evening for problems, and I kept a notebook where I wrote out solutions in full. In the beginning, I could solve only two or three problems in a session. A full problem set on number theory, I could finish it in the same time by the end of the third month. The routine felt boring at first, but it built up a base that I could rely on when the problems got harder.

The second thing I learned was how to read my own mistakes. My teacher suggested that I mark every wrong answer with a short note about what went wrong, whether it was a careless arithmetic slip, a missing case, or a wrong assumption. Reading the same at the end of each week showed me a pattern. I was losing marks on the same kind of error again and again, and the notes forced me to slow down at that step in future problems.

The third thing I learned was the value of working with a classmate. Once a week, my classmate and I met at the school library and worked through problems together on a whiteboard. Explaining a step out loud was harder than writing it down, and my classmate often asked a question that made me see a gap in my reasoning. This changed the way I approached practice on my own, because I began to ask myself the same kind of questions only.

The competition itself did not go the way I had hoped. I solved fewer problems than I had expected, and I have made a careless error on a question I had practised many times. For a few days afterwards I felt that the whole effort had been wasted. Looking back now, I do not think that anymore. The habits I built in those six months carried over into how I studied for my final school exams and the engineering entrance exam. I also learned that a single result does not define what I have gained, and that a long stretch of quiet work is often more valuable than one high pressure test.

I want to continue studying mathematics as part of my engineering degree, and I hope to work on problems that combine mathematical reasoning with computer science. The competition taught me that I can face a hard subject if I am willing to build up my understanding one small small step at a time. I am ready to take that approach into university.
