For a long time, I believed that being right in an argument was the same as understanding a topic. In my penultimate year of school, I lost a class debate on whether social media does more harm than good to teenagers. I had argued the harmful side with confidence; by the end of the hour, however, I no longer agreed with my own position. The experience taught me that changing my mind after listening carefully is a serious form of learning rather than a minor defeat.

The first thing that surprised me was how superficial my preparation had been. I had read articles that supported my view and quoted statistics without verifying them. The lead speaker on the opposing team, a reserved classmate with whom I had rarely spoken, opened by asking the audience to name one thing they had learned online in the past month. The room filled with answers about tutorials, science channels, and long-distance study groups. I had prepared nothing to counter this. In that moment, I realised that I had constructed my case from headlines rather than from real experience.

The second thing that shifted my view was a specific argument concerning access. My classmate pointed out that in many households, a single phone is the only reliable means of reaching teachers, applying for scholarships, or attending online classes when schools are closed. I had grown accustomed to regarding social media as a distraction, because that was how it functioned in my own home. Consequently, I had not considered the students for whom the same platforms served as a route into education. That gap in my thinking was evident to the entire room.

The third thing was smaller, yet it remained with me the longest. At the end of the session, my teacher asked each of us to write down one point from the opposing side that we found convincing. I wrote three, and then a fourth. I had lost the debate; nevertheless, I could not stop thinking about the arguments I had failed to answer.

In the weeks that followed, I did what I had neglected to do before the debate. I read articles and research that disagreed with me. I asked my parent how communication had changed since their own school years. Moreover, I spoke to my classmate again and thanked them for the preparation they had evidently undertaken. Gradually, I understood that my earlier position had been not so much wrong as narrow. Careful thinking is uncomfortable because it requires letting go of your first opinion and forming a new one from evidence.

I would like to study the social sciences at university because I wish to continue having that experience. I hope to work on questions where the evidence is mixed and clear answers are rare. Losing that debate taught me that a good education is not about defending fixed positions, but about being willing to listen and to change my mind.
