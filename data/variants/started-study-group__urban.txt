During the year of my final school examinations, I observed that studying alone was making me slower rather than faster. Consequently, I resolved to form a small study group with three classmates, and sustaining it for eight months taught me more about learning than any individual subject did. My principal realisation was that a study group is not about pooling knowledge; rather, it is about establishing a routine from which no one can easily withdraw.

Our first step was to fix a venue and a schedule. We met at the school library from four to six on weekdays. I created a shared notebook in which each of us recorded what we intended to cover before the next meeting. Although this may seem minor, it was precisely why the group did not disband within the first month. When one classmate wished to skip a session because of a family visit, the notebook made explicit what would be missed. Such accountability proved more useful than any motivational talk from me.

The second measure was rotating the responsibility for explaining topics. Initially, one classmate who excelled in physics did most of the teaching, and I noticed that the rest of us had begun to depend on that single voice. I therefore proposed that each of us take one chapter per week and prepare a brief explanation. This was uncomfortable, since I had to teach a chemistry chapter that I had barely understood. Preparing to explain it obliged me to read it three times. I am now convinced that teaching a topic is the quickest way to learn it thoroughly.

The third measure was keeping the group small. When two other students asked to join in the third month, I declined. I was apprehensive about appearing rude; however, I had seen larger groups degenerate into social gatherings. Four members was the appropriate size for our schedule, and preserving that mattered more than being polite. My teacher later remarked that this had been a mature decision, which surprised me, because at the time it had felt selfish.

Running this group taught me that discipline does not arise from motivation. Rather, it stems from small structures that make quitting harder than continuing. I once regarded studying as a private activity, and I still do most of my reading alone. Nevertheless, I now recognise that other people can serve as scaffolding, and that removing them at the wrong moment causes the entire plan to collapse. I also learned that I am not naturally organised; the notebook, the fixed hours and the rotation were all attempts to compensate for that.

I am currently preparing for the engineering entrance examination, and I intend to study computer science at university. I am aware that college work will be more demanding and more independent. Accordingly, I plan to establish similar structures early, so that I need not rely on motivation alone. The habit of meeting, preparing and explaining is one I wish to carry forward.
