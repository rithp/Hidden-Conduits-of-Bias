When I began preparing for the engineering entrance examination, my parent informed me plainly that we could not afford a coaching class. I recall feeling anxious for about a week. Thereafter, I resolved that if I could not purchase the standard preparation, I would have to construct my own. That decision, more than any individual study session, is what I wish to write about here. In retrospect, the process of assembling my preparation from small, freely available components taught me how to approach problems that do not come with a ready solution.

The first thing I did was to list what a coaching class actually provides. I arrived at three items: a set of practice problems, a schedule that ensures continued progress, and someone to explain a topic when one is stuck. I decided to address each of these separately.

For practice problems, I visited my school library after classes and photographed old preparation books that were falling apart. My parent bought me one recent guidebook, which I used as my principal text. Whenever a topic was unclear in the newer book, I searched for the same topic in the older ones. Between them, I had sufficient problems to work through every day.

For the schedule, I wrote a plain weekly plan on a sheet of paper and affixed it above my desk. Each week, I ticked off whatever I had completed. I kept the plan simple, since I knew that a complicated plan would not survive a difficult week. Consequently, if I fell behind, I did not rewrite the plan; I simply resumed from where I had stopped.

For the third item, someone to explain difficult topics, I asked my school teacher whether I could stay back and ask questions. My teacher agreed to give me time twice a week. Moreover, I formed a small study group with two classmates who were preparing for the same examination. We took turns explaining chapters to one another. Explaining a topic aloud, in simple language, enabled me to identify gaps in my own understanding.

In retrospect, the most difficult part was not the studying itself. Rather, it was accepting that my preparation would differ from that of my classmates. For a considerable period, I felt that I was falling behind because I lacked the same materials and the same test series. I later understood that the comparison itself was the real problem. Once I stopped measuring my preparation against theirs and began measuring it against the syllabus, I could recognise that I was, in fact, making steady progress.

I wish to study engineering because I appreciate the discipline of breaking a large problem into smaller ones and solving each independently. That is precisely what I did with my own preparation for the examination, and I would like to learn to do it better, with more advanced tools, alongside other students who are likewise attempting to solve difficult problems.
